verilog carries the largest damage figure on the board - 63,937 bytes - and
until today contributed **zero adjudicable bytes**. Three lanes worked it
anyway, off `damage`, which is `size − built`: outliner's own words about its own
forest, and a number a root stretched over a hole buys outright. Nothing on the
board said the row could not be scored, because there was no column for it.

**`standing.py` grows an `unjudg` column**, beside `crooked` rather than in a
footnote. **`rack run` grows a `NOT JUDGED` block** that fires on the *share*
rather than on the mechanism. The block it replaces was keyed on `Seen.why` -
the grammar the oracle refused whole - and failed in both directions: verilog
was refused whole and got one line under a heading softer than the fact, while a
row silent over a seventh of its bytes got nothing at all, because `why` is
empty when the bytes were refused one at a time by `plumb`'s rule rather than by
the reader giving up. Any row over 5% blind is now named, with its share, and a
whole refusal is called out separately because that is precisely the case where
`damage` is the only instrument left and a work order will use it regardless.

The 12,579 unframed are 337 distinct spans, of which the single widest
(`module_declaration` over 69,204 bytes, against our 3,544 roots) is 8,573 and is
charged separately as the forest-versus-tree difference; the other 4,006 are a
construct-by-construct absence led by `module_ansi_header`,
`list_of_port_declarations` and sixteen `ansi_port_declaration`s. The 12,112
racked name one confusion: we build `list_of_variable_decl_assignments` where
tree-sitter builds `blocking_assignment`, and `list_of_net_decl_assignments`
where it builds `list_of_net_assignments`. We are reading procedural assignments
as declaration lists. That is a fork-selection question and belongs to the press
lane; it is recorded here because no instrument on this tree could name it
before.

**`damage` is confirmed to the byte and was never the question.** The two
standing claims against it - 8,175 bytes of unfixable grammar limitation and
49,446 bytes of four procedural-block defects - are both claims about the 63,937
bytes verilog never built, and are silent about the 30,720 it did. At verilog's
own measured rate, converting damage into built is worth **two square bytes per
hundred**. A work order ranked on `damage` alone is ranking on the one number
that has never been checked against another parser, on the row where that check
turns out worst.
